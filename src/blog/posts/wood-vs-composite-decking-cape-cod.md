Choosing deck boards is one of the first decisions in any deck project, and one of the ones homeowners ask us about most. There's no single right answer. The best choice depends on how you use the deck, how much upkeep you want to do, your budget and how exposed the house is.

Here's how the common options compare on Cape Cod.

## The main options

**Pressure-treated pine** is the most budget-friendly. It's strong and widely available, but it needs regular cleaning and staining or sealing, and it's prone to checking and splintering as it ages.

**Cedar** looks warm, weathers to grey like cedar shingles, and is naturally rot-resistant. It's softer than other options, so it dents more easily, and it still needs periodic finishing to keep its color.

**Tropical hardwoods** such as ipe are very dense and long-lasting, with a rich look. They cost more, are harder to work with, and still need oiling if you want to keep the color rather than let them grey.

**Composite and PVC boards** cost more up front but don't need staining or sealing. They resist rot and splinters and mostly need washing. Quality varies a lot between product lines, so the brand and series matter.

## How they compare

**Upfront cost.** Pressure-treated is lowest, cedar and composite in the middle to upper range depending on the product, hardwood at the top.

**Maintenance.** Wood decks need cleaning and re-finishing every year or two to look their best. Composite needs washing, and occasionally attention to mildew in shady spots.

**Heat.** Composite boards, especially darker colors, get hotter in direct summer sun than wood. On a south-facing deck used barefoot, lighter colors help.

**Salt air and sun.** All decking weathers on the coast. Composite holds its color better than unfinished wood, but quality varies. On any deck near the water, fasteners and railing hardware should be stainless or coated for coastal use.

**Look.** Some homeowners want real wood, especially on traditional or historic homes. Others prefer the clean, consistent look of composite.

## The framing matters more than the boards

Whatever goes on top, the structure underneath is almost always pressure-treated wood. That's where decks really fail: the ledger connection to the house, joists, footings, stair stringers and railing posts.

A few things to know:

- **Composite often needs closer joist spacing** than wood, especially for diagonal layouts. Follow the board manufacturer's spec.
- **Replacing only the boards** makes sense when the framing is sound. If it isn't, new boards on an old frame is money spent twice.
- **Flashing and drainage** at the ledger keep water from rotting the house rim joist.
- **Railings and stairs** need to be solid and built to code. Safety issues come before anything cosmetic.

## Which is right for you?

- **Rental cottage or high-traffic deck:** composite, for low upkeep and fewer splinters. We see this often on summer rentals in [Dennis](/service-areas/dennis-ma/) and on the Outer Cape.
- **Tight budget, willing to maintain it:** pressure-treated, finished well and kept up.
- **Traditional home, want the real wood look:** cedar or hardwood.
- **Waterfront or very exposed:** quality composite or hardwood, with stainless fasteners and coastal-rated railing hardware.
- **Newer home that already has PVC trim:** composite usually matches the low-maintenance approach. It's common in neighborhoods like the Pinehills in [Plymouth](/service-areas/plymouth-ma/).
